Kishore Biyani

The man who brought the Indian bazaar indoors: founder of Pantaloons and Big Bazaar, and the face of the rise and fall of Future Group.

Born
Mumbai (Bombay), India
Known for
Founding Pantaloons, Big Bazaar and Future Group
Industry
Retail
Education
Bachelor's degree, Bombay University (H.R. College)

From trousers to Pantaloons

Kishore Biyani grew up in Mumbai and studied at H.R. College. Rather than join his family's business, he started his own in the 1980s, spotting a demand for stone-washed trousers among young men. Forbes dates the start of his retail story to 1987, when he launched a trouser brand called Pantaloons. It grew into a large fashion retailer, and its first big format store opened in Kolkata.

Big Bazaar and the Indian middle class

In 2001 Biyani opened the first Big Bazaar stores. He ignored the advice to copy Western retail and designed the stores to look slightly chaotic, like the traditional bazaars his customers already knew, with low prices, bundled offers and a wide range under one roof. He has said luck played a part: his ambitious ideas arrived just as India's middle class had more money to spend. By 2009 there were over 100 Big Bazaar stores serving about two million customers a week, and Pantaloon Retail employed more than 30,000 people across about 1,000 stores in 71 cities. The group's turnover in 2008 was around ₹4,700 crore.

Expansion and the 2008 crisis

Through Future Group he expanded into food retail, electronics, home, financial services, insurance, media and even films. Much of this was funded by short-term borrowing, and Pantaloon Retail's debt-to-equity ratio reached about 3:1. When Lehman Brothers collapsed in September 2008, sales fell, lenders called in loans, and the group's market value dropped by about two-thirds in six months. Biyani cut mid-level management, rolled over debt into longer loans, pulled out of joint ventures, narrowed the group's focus from more than 20 formats to four (fashion, food, home and general merchandise), brought in McKinsey, and handed day-to-day retail operations to his cousin Rakesh Biyani.

Selling Pantaloons

By April 2012 the group was again under pressure over debt, and in May 2012 Aditya Birla Nuvo took a controlling stake in Pantaloon Retail through a demerger. The business was later renamed Aditya Birla Fashion and Retail. Biyani then rebuilt around Future Retail, which relisted as an asset-light retailer in 2016 and, in 2019, signed a franchise deal to bring 7-Eleven stores to India.

The Reliance deal and the collapse of Future Retail

In August 2020 Reliance Retail agreed to acquire the retail and wholesale business of Future Group in a deal reported at about $3.4 billion. The deal was contested, and Future Retail later entered insolvency proceedings. Reports in April 2026 said lease termination notices had been served on several hundred Future Retail stores, and around 200 of the group's stores, including Big Bazaar outlets, had earlier been converted to Smart Bazaar.

Regulatory action and the aftermath

In April 2024 Biyani sold the oldest of his malls, SOBO Central in Mumbai, to K Raheja Corp for ₹476 crore as part of settling a debt with Canara Bank. In May 2026 India's market regulator SEBI fined Biyani, Rakesh Biyani and another former Future Retail executive over disclosure and governance lapses. He has also described his own style as sometimes "whimsical", and critics point to over-diversification and heavy debt as the main causes of the group's troubles.

A new chapter: The Foundery

In 2026 Biyani co-founded The Foundery, a 90-day startup incubator, with entrepreneur Nikhil Kamath, marking his return to business. He is also the co-author, with Dipayan Baishya, of It Happened in India, which in 2007 was the best-selling business book ever published in India, with sales above 100,000 copies.

Recognition

  • EY Entrepreneur of the Year, Business Transformation category (2018)
  • Retailer of the Year, National Retail Federation (India)
  • Forbes India's Richest list (2019): about $1.78 billion net worth, at the time
The takeaway: design for how your customers already shop, then guard the balance sheet as fiercely as you grow. Biyani built retail that felt familiar to millions of Indians, but expansion funded by debt left no cushion when the market turned.
